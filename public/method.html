How the birds learn

There's no training data and no gradient descent here. Each bird is a small neural network described by a genome. The whole population flies the same course, the ones that get furthest have more children, and the children come out slightly different. Repeat that a few dozen times and you get birds that can fly. The method is NEAT (Stanley and Miikkulainen, 2002), and all of it runs in your browser.

What a bird sees

Four numbers, every frame: how far it is above or below the next gap, how fast it's moving up or down, how far it still has to go to clear the next pipe, and where the gap after that one is. That last one turned out to matter once the gaps get tight, because a bird that only knows about the next gap tends to come out of it heading the wrong way.

The network has one output. If it's above 0.5, the bird flaps.

Networks that grow

Every bird starts as the simplest possible network, with the four inputs wired straight to the output. When a bird has children, most of them get their weights nudged. About one in seven also gets a new connection, and about one in seventeen gets a new hidden node, which is added by splitting an existing connection in two.

Each new connection gets a number that's shared across the whole run, so if two birds happen to grow the same connection, it has the same number in both. That's what lets two differently shaped parents have a child that makes sense: their genes line up by number.

Species

A new node usually makes a bird a bit worse before it makes it better. If every bird competed against every other bird, those new ideas would die out straight away. So NEAT sorts birds into species by how different their genes are, and each species gets a share of the next generation based on how well its members did on average. A big species can't crowd out a small one just by being big.

A species that hasn't improved in 15 generations gets dropped, unless it's one of the two best. The Species setting picks roughly how many species to aim for.

The course

The first gap is always in the middle. After that the gaps shrink from 176 px to 122 px over the first 60 pipes, and they're allowed to jump further up or down from one to the next. Early on, almost any sensible bird gets somewhere, and later it takes real control. With 50 birds, a run usually clears all 100 pipes somewhere between generation 3 and 17.

Each generation gets a new course unless you set it to the same one, in which case the birds can end up memorising it. Play mode uses exactly the same physics and courses, so your score and the ghost's are comparable.
